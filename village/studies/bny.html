AI Platform Design

Giving a growing AI platform a coherent structure

BNY · Senior (Staff) AI Designer · via Blink · Oct 2025 to Present

BNY's AI products had grown faster than the experience connecting them. I lead design for Eliza's core experience. I proposed the hierarchical information architecture the team adopted, led its navigation redesign through user testing, and partnered with engineering on the production rollout. I also created the adopted task model for how work reaches people; the broader experience built on it is still in validation.

This work is under NDA. Examples are limited to the design reasoning and permitted outcomes.

Make the platform understandable beyond its familiar parts

In one usability session, a daily user told me he'd stopped exploring because navigation was too confusing. Other sessions exposed a similar problem: people couldn't reliably tell what a destination was or where a link would take them. Adding access to more products wasn't enough.

I proposed a hierarchical information architecture to give the growing platform a consistent structure. The tradeoff was depth: grouping destinations creates room to grow, but it can also bury what someone uses every day. I ran 1:1 interviews and usability sessions to test those choices, then brought the findings back into the design.

Testing caught a mistake I'd made. A navigation icon I chose read as Safari, so its purpose wasn't clear. I revised the icon choice. A participant also lost their sense of place when opening the menu changed the surrounding navigation; I revised that interaction so exploring destinations preserved the current context.

The feedback wasn't uniform. One participant found pinning easily; another couldn't discover it or tell whether it had worked. I treated that as an unresolved discoverability problem. Some personalization changes were deferred beyond the navigation release.

The team adopted the IA, and the global navigation shipped to production across BNY's AI applications in July 2026. That is the confirmed outcome. I don't have a measured improvement in findability to attach to it.

Show where work comes from before asking people to act

The next problem arrived as a notifications requirement. People needed to pick up unfinished work and understand what needed their attention. A feed ordered by arrival time couldn't answer that on its own.

I created a task model with three origins: work you create yourself, work assigned to you, and work AI proposes. The distinction matters because a suggestion from AI carries a different expectation from an assignment. I made the source part of the model, so people could judge what they were being asked to do.

The model was adopted and tested in 1:1 sessions. I then used it to explore prioritization and acting with enough context to avoid repeatedly leaving the work at hand. Early reviews exposed the next constraint: a combined list can become overwhelming when it meets real workloads. Consolidating work doesn't automatically make it manageable.

I'm validating that broader experience across different work styles. The open question is what deserves attention for each person, and what context they need before acting. The adopted taxonomy is a foundation for that work, not evidence that the whole experience has shipped.

My wider scope includes the agent marketplace, the assistant's interaction design, and visual direction across the core experience. I also shaped design-system strategy before handing implementation to a colleague during paternity leave. Those responsibilities support the same work: keeping the experience coherent as the platform grows.
