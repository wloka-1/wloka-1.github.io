Design Systems

Shared rules for three different brands

Qualcomm · Senior (Staff) Product Designer, Design Systems Lead · via Blink · Feb to Sept 2025

Qualcomm, Snapdragon, and Dragonwing needed a shared design system that could serve both marketing and complex product tools. I set visual direction, wrote design principles with the team, and led iconography, pattern research, and documentation architecture. We shipped the enterprise beta with 60+ component documents and an icon library of 1,500+. The system is now public.

Unify the rules without flattening the brands

A utility icon beside a table row and a marketing illustration have different jobs. Product teams needed small, legible controls. Marketing needed more expressive artwork. Putting both into one library meant defining where their rules should differ.

I established separate sizing and stroke ramps for utility and marketing icons, tied to shared foundations. Utility icons used predefined sizes and alignment rules. Marketing icons kept their larger scale, illustrative strokes, and greater color flexibility. Those distinctions let the library support all three brands without making every icon behave the same way.

The sizing guidance makes the rule concrete: choose from the defined ramp instead of scaling an icon by eye. Size and stroke weight stay coordinated.

Custom icons also needed a cost of entry. I defined a review path: check that no existing icon or substitute works, establish multiple uses, then get system-team approval. The library shipped, but finding the right icon still had friction. At the first-version review, adding search metadata to 1,500+ entries was too much manual work; designers still relied on the source library's search. Shared standards were in place. Discovery wasn't solved.

Let product differences define the pattern guidance

Component specs couldn't answer the questions product designers brought to me. In an IoT product, should filters live above a table or inside its columns? When a creation form has too many fields, does it still belong in a modal? Teams needed guidance on how components work together across a task.

I interviewed designers and design leadership, collected examples from shipping products, and compared them across navigation, forms, and tables. Navigation and data tables emerged as recurring needs. I used that research to define the pattern framework and get leadership agreement on the roadmap.

The synthesis groups recurring needs across products. Navigation and data-heavy interfaces gave us concrete places to start.

The side-navigation audit compares hierarchy, nesting, and collapsed states. A shared component alone wouldn't resolve those differences.

The choice was to separate reusable layouts from guidance about when and how to use them. That gave teams room to handle different information densities and workflows while working from common rules. The research established the framework and priorities; it wasn't a finished pattern library for every product.

Make the documentation demonstrate the system

I owned the documentation structure and page templates, then worked with engineering on the custom storefront. I also drew the component illustrations by hand. The shared grid and connector-line logic gave very different components a consistent visual language. Engineering rebuilt those illustrations in code so they could respond to brand themes and light and dark modes.

My component illustrations in the published system. The repeated grid and connectors hold the family together while each drawing explains a different component.

Getting the content into that storefront was another constraint. Manual transfer took roughly two hours per page. I built a Claude-assisted workflow for drafting documentation and converting it into CMS content, with designers reviewing and refining the output. The team delivered 60+ component documents for enterprise beta. Automation helped us fill the site; the structure, examples, and review still needed design judgment.
